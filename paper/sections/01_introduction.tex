\section{Introduction}
Recovering dense metric scene geometry from an image is a fundamental problem in computer vision. While modern monocular models exhibit strong recognition of object shape and scene layout, a single photograph does not uniquely determine metric scale. An active depth sensor provides an additional geometric cue, yet practical mobile sensors often deliver noisy, low-resolution, or spatially incomplete range measurements. This motivates RGB-guided depth completion: infer a dense metric field from appearance while respecting the evidence available from a depth sensor. The ability to obtain spatially detailed metric depth has direct implications for mapping, augmented reality, and robotic systems that must reason about surfaces and free space.

Early learning-based systems established that even a limited number of depth observations can significantly constrain the reconstruction of a dense scene~\cite{ma2018sparse}. Subsequent propagation-based approaches learned spatial relationships and confidence-aware affinities to improve the distribution of reliable measurements over missing regions~\cite{cheng2018cspn,park2020nlspn}. More recent architectures combine convolutional locality with global representations~\cite{zhang2023completionformer}. A common property of these methods is that the fusion procedure is learned for the downstream task, typically with access to a substantial depth-completion training set. This is effective, but does not directly answer how best to adapt a \emph{pretrained prompt-conditioned depth foundation model} to a different sensor or scene distribution.

Foundation-model pretraining changes the starting point of this problem. Representations learned from large image corpora have improved dense perception~\cite{oquab2024dinov2,yang2024depthanything,yang2024depthanythingv2}. Prompt Depth Anything (\base)~\cite{lin2025promptda} extends this direction by combining a strong visual predictor with metric depth prompts. Unlike a network that must infer both layout and scale from scratch, a prompt-conditioned estimator can use the sensor input to anchor its metric prediction. Its output is therefore an attractive starting point for local adaptation: a downstream module need not relearn generic scene geometry if most of the pretrained estimate is already accurate.

However, refining an already accurate depth map poses a distinct challenge. The desired change is often small and spatially nonuniform. A uniformly expressive residual network may correct genuine errors but can also disturb pixels that the pretrained model already estimates well. Conversely, suppressing all corrections near image edges is not necessarily desirable, because appearance edges can reflect texture changes rather than changes in geometric depth. The main practical question is therefore not simply whether a postprocessing head can reduce training loss, but \emph{where it should act, how strongly it should modify the coarse prediction, and whether that selectivity is more useful than a conventional learned residual}.

We explore this question through spatially adaptive consistency-gated refinement (\method). Our design separates the estimation of a candidate metric correction from the estimation of its spatial weight. The correction path uses RGB observations, the frozen coarse depth, and a feature tensor from the pretrained depth decoder. The gate uses a normalized RGB--depth gradient agreement map, the coarse depth, and a learned spatial field based on the validity pattern of the original low-resolution LiDAR measurements. The refined depth is formed by adding the gated correction to the original prediction. Importantly, the adapter does \emph{not} fine-tune the pretrained weights. The metric values of the LiDAR input influence the frozen predictor, while the field provided to the adapter encodes observation locations; it is not a second depth-value fusion module.

This design is motivated by two observations rather than an assumption of calibrated confidence. First, an image edge that aligns with a depth edge is different evidence from an image edge with no corresponding predicted geometric transition. Second, the spatial arrangement of valid range measurements provides information about where the original prediction has direct sensor support. Neither observation, by itself, reveals the correct depth or guarantees that a pixel is reliable. Accordingly, the gate should be understood as a \emph{learned consistency-conditioned modulation field}, not as a probability of correctness.

Our original SACG training objective contained valid-pixel L1 error, an exponential RGB-edge-weighted boundary term, and delayed gate regularization. In the available ARKitScenes seed-10 comparison, removing the boundary-weighted term improves L1 and absolute relative error (AbsRel) relative to the original objective. We therefore treat this \emph{no-boundary-loss variant} as the working main configuration. The name refers to the training objective: the model still computes image--depth gradients and still uses those gradients to condition its gate. We do not interpret the observed loss-ablation outcome as a general finding that boundaries are unimportant for depth completion.

The empirical evidence is deliberately reported with a strong control and a clear distinction between development and final evaluation. On the same 5,599 ARKitScenes validation frames, a separately trained plain residual CNN performs better than full SACG on several metrics. Our selected no-boundary configuration achieves lower L1 and AbsRel than that CNN, but the differences are small; the CNN still obtains lower RMSE and lower RGB-edge-based boundary error. Three-seed and ScanNet++ results stored in the repository concern \emph{the earlier full-objective version}, not the selected model. Consequently, a conclusion about general superiority, robustness or cross-dataset transfer would be premature.

The contributions of this working study are threefold. \textbf{First}, we formulate and implement a lightweight, frozen-backbone refinement design that decouples candidate metric corrections from spatial consistency-conditioned gating. \textbf{Second}, we analyze a learnable LiDAR-validity field and a simple objective choice in the context of an already accurate pretrained depth model, distinguishing spatial support from the metric values of sparse depth. \textbf{Third}, we provide traceable, paired validation evidence against a plain CNN and several ablations, together with an explicit account of the controls and independent tests needed to establish an architectural advantage. We emphasize that the first two points describe design choices being investigated; novelty relative to the full contemporary literature and reliable superiority still require broader verification.
